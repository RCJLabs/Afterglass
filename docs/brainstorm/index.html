The problem with the pitch

As written, it's already on the Play Store twice.

The Elder Scrolls: Castles (Bethesda Game Studios, global launch September 2024) is Fallout Shelter in an Elder Scrolls castle, made by the Fallout Shelter team. Hustle Castle (MY.GAMES, 2017) is a side-view medieval Fallout Shelter with invasions and PvP raids. Craft the World, Kingdom Two Crowns, Hammerting, Grow Castle and Castle Doombad cover most of the rest, and Kingdom III is due in 2027.

The opening: Castles has no castle defense at all (its battles happen away from the castle), and Hustle Castle's defense is a free-to-play PvP layer. A fantasy setting and a side view won't set you apart. A defense mechanic that only works in cross-section can.

Recommendation

Hoardkeep, with Countermine's tunnel war as its defense.

You build on a sleeping dragon, and everything makes noise: your forges, your gold-skimming, and the enemy's tunnels. You defend by listening for their digging. Your own noise risks waking the dragon, and waking it on purpose is your last-resort weapon. It has the clearest hook of the five, fits a portrait phone, and one person can build it.

  • If PC/Steam is the first market instead, build pure Countermine. It's the most novel of the five; the search found no game built around it.
  • Before committing, run the two one-week greybox fun-tests and drop whichever fails its kill criteria.
  • The ranking depends on my weights. Change them if your priorities differ.

What I assumed about you

These drive the scope and platform scores. If one is wrong, the ranking changes.

One developer, web stack

Canvas or WebGL in the browser, no native engine. Web-stack games have shipped on Steam: Vampire Survivors launched on Phaser, and CrossCode shipped as HTML5 inside NW.js.

PWA first, TWA on Play

GitHub Pages for the web build, a Trusted Web Activity for Google Play. TWAs can take payments through Play Billing using the Digital Goods API.

Art one person can make

Low-res pixel art. The mockups below use 320×180 and 180×320 base resolutions, which is cheap to animate and forgiving of rough drawing.

No servers at launch

That rules out async PvP raids and any design that needs live ops to stay fair, which is where Hustle Castle lives.

Countermine

Hold the walls. Win the war beneath them.

A besieging army arrives, and relief is forty days away. Above ground it's a conventional siege: walls, archers, trebuchets, rationing. Below ground their sappers are digging toward your foundations, and you can't see them. You listen for them, dig countermines to cut them off, and fight in the dark.

Core loop, one siege day

  1. Dawn report: stores, damage, and what the listening posts heard overnight.
  2. Set the day's orders: rations, shifts, where to dig, what to repair.
  3. The day runs in real time with pause: bombardment, repairs, digging, tunnel fights.
  4. Night brings assaults, sorties, sickness and desertion checks.
  5. Win by lasting until relief arrives, or by wrecking enough engines and morale to break the siege.

The new part: fog of war underground

  • Enemy tunnels stay hidden until detected. A listening post gives a direction and a distance band; a scrying mage can reveal one segment, at a price. Listening for mines is historical: Herodotus describes a smith at the siege of Barca finding Persian tunnels by tapping a bronze shield, and WWI tunnellers used geophones.
  • You choose where to dig. When galleries meet, it's a tunnel fight or a dirty trick: smoke, flooding from the moat, or pulling the props to bury the sappers and weaken the ground above.
  • A sap that reaches your foundations gets fired. The props burn, that stretch of wall falls, and the assault comes through the breach at night.
  • Every worker underground is one not repairing walls or tending the kitchen garden. That labor split is the whole game.

Threats

Goblin sapper hordes that dig many shallow saps. An undead legion that never eats and never breaks, but moves slowly. Renegade legionaries with heavy engines. Wyvern riders setting roofs alight. Plague and deserters.

Structure

Roguelite. One siege is one run of about an hour, with a different castle and besieger each time. Between runs you unlock castellan traits, castle layouts and engineering doctrines.

Hoardkeep

Build on a dragon. Try not to wake it.

Your clan's keep sits on the mountain where a dragon sleeps on its hoard. Its breath heats your forges, its shed scales armor your guards, and its gold pays for everything. Every hammer blow and every skimmed coin makes it stir. Dragonslayers want its head, adventurers want its gold, and your clan is all that stands between them and it. When you're about to be overrun, you can wake it on purpose.

Core loop

  1. Dig rooms into the mountain and staff them.
  2. Produce: forges, kitchens, trap workshops. Production makes noise.
  3. Keep the stir down with tapestries and moss, lullaby bards, feeding, and by timing loud work to its deepest sleep.
  4. Skim the hoard for fast gold and a big spike in stir.
  5. Defend. Parties walk in through the adits and dragonslayer sappers dig toward the lair. You have traps, guards, and vents that channel its breath.
  6. At 100% stir, or when you choose, it wakes and burns everything near its path, attackers first. Pay a tribute to lull it back to sleep, or it leaves and takes the hoard.

The new part: one noise system, three jobs

  • Your noise travels through the rock to the lair, and you can see the ripples. Depth is the trade-off: rooms near the lair get dragon heat and hoard access, but the dragon hears them better.
  • Their noise gives them away. Sappers digging toward the hoard are heard the same way; this is Countermine's tunnel war. Digging a countermine near the lair is loud, so every interception risks the wake.
  • Its breathing is a clock. Flame vents fire on the exhale, so trap corridors pulse on a rhythm you can plan around.
  • The dragon grows with its hoard: more heat, more power, more danger.
  • Waking it is a weapon. Timing a wake for the moment the dragonslayers break through is the signature play.

Who you are

A kobold clan (small, funny, good with traps, and the easiest to market) or a dragon-cult noble house (closer to the dragon priests of the Elder Scrolls). The mockup uses kobolds.

Threats

Adventurer parties on loot runs. A dragonslayer order whose crusades escalate. Rival clans raiding by tunnel. A necromancer who wants the bones. Cave-ins.

Structure

A persistent keep, like Fallout Shelter, with seasonal hunts and a long arc: the dragon ages, and eventually it's slain, it leaves, or it ascends. Short sessions work.

Side view
Strong. Noise traveling down through rock to the dragon, and depth as risk, only read in section.
Novelty
GoodThe combination looks new; the parts don't. Don't Wake the Beast (announced 2024) has a noise-driven awakening meter in a stealth roguelite, Clank! raises a dragon's rage with noise, and an itch.io "Dragon Game" has you direct kobolds as the dragon.
Scope
MediumFor 1.0: one mountain with procedural variation, about 16 room types, 6 enemy types, the dragon (one big sprite plus the wake set piece), about 50 events, the tunnel war.
Platform
Portrait with short sessions. A persistent keep suits phones and offline progress.
Risks
Players may hold stir near zero forever; a hunger cycle that forces periodic wakes fixes that, and a deliberate wake must sometimes be the best move. Expect Dungeon Keeper comparisons. The wake sequence is the one expensive art piece.
Prototype asks
Does greed against noise force a real decision every minute, and does a deliberate wake feel like a gamble that paid off?

Mirrorhold

Every room you build has a reflection. Something lives down there.

Your keep stands on the Veil, the thin skin between the waking world and the Umbra. Everything you build above ground also appears upside down beneath it, as a shadow keep worked by your people's shadows while they sleep. By day you run the castle and fight raiders. By night you run its reflection and hold back what lives in the dark.

Core loop

  1. Day: build rooms (each one creates its twin), assign workers, produce materials, fight off raiders.
  2. Night: your sleepers' shadows work the twins and produce essence. Wards and lanterns hold off the umbral horrors.
  3. Spend essence on wards, enchanted gear and rituals. Drawing essence thins the Veil.
  4. Damage crosses over. A fallen twin haunts its real room; a burned real room leaves its twin adrift.

The new part: a mirrored base

  • One build action, two rooms. Layout matters twice: a room that's safe above can be exposed below, where the reflection's rooftops face the deep.
  • Day and night alternate your focus, so you never run both realms at full intensity at once. On a phone screen that halves the load.
  • The Veil is your risk budget. Strong magic thins it, and thin spots let things climb into the real keep.

Threats

Above: raiders, bandit lords, a jealous neighbor. Below: shades, gloams, the drowned. When the Veil thins, both sides spill over.

Structure

A persistent keep or a campaign, with a day–night cycle of about six to eight minutes.

Side view
Essential. The ground line is the mirror.
Novelty
StrongClosest is Parallel Empires, a small itch.io game where each building spawns an upside-down copy in a mirror world, but that copy is an enemy and damage doesn't carry across. Flip TD flips the world to defend both sides.
Name
My first title was Veilkeep, but Veilkeeper (PocketLore Studios, a tactical RPG with base building) is too close. Mirrorhold is a placeholder and hasn't been checked.
Scope
Medium–largeTwin rooms double the design work. They don't double the art if the reflection is a palette-and-shimmer effect like the mockup's.
Platform
Portrait. The keep above and its reflection below fill a tall screen.
Risks
The hardest of the five to explain in a sentence, though a screenshot explains itself. The night half could become a chore instead of a second game.

Hollow Colossus

Your castle is inside a dead god-machine. Wake it.

A brass colossus from a vanished civilization kneels in the wastes, dead for a thousand years. Your settlers move in. Each sealed chamber you open gives you space and ancient machinery, and wakes more of its immune system: guardian constructs that treat you as an infection. Restore its heart, then its limbs. Eventually it stands up, and your castle walks.

Core loop

  1. Open sealed chambers in a fixed anatomy: head, chest, arms, belly, legs.
  2. Claim, furnish and staff them. Repair its systems: heart furnace, hydraulics, optics.
  3. Handle the alarm. Guardians spawn from waking nodes; destroy them or reprogram them into defenders.
  4. Fight off scavengers who want its brass.
  5. Once the legs work, walk. Travel changes the biome, the resources and the threats, and raiders chase you and try to board.

The new part: the base is a body

  • The body is the map. Every chamber is hand-authored, with its own shape and machinery.
  • Alarm is the risk budget. The deeper you tamper, the more of the colossus fights you.
  • Reprogramming turns the internal enemy into your garrison.
  • The walking phase turns a base builder into a journey.

Threats

Guardian constructs, scavenger clans, wyvern riders, sandstorms. Late game, other colossi, which are also a scope trap.

Structure

A campaign in three acts: settle, restore, walk.

Side view
Strong. The body's silhouette is the map, and its anatomy stacks vertically.
Novelty
ModerateBases on walking giants are now a small genre: The Wandering Village (top-down, on a creature's back), Ark of Charon (2024; golem workers turn a walking World-Tree creature into a fortress) and Beyond These Stars (a city on a space whale). Settling inside a dormant machine whose guardians act as an immune system didn't turn up.
Scope
LargeThe walking phase is nearly a second game: parallax travel, biomes, chase fights. Cut it and the concept loses its payoff.
Platform
Portrait. A standing giant fits a phone screen.
Risks
Scope. Fixed chamber shapes remove the free room layout that makes Fallout Shelter-likes satisfying. It drifts from castle fantasy toward steampunk mech.

Deadhold

They keep attacking. You keep hiring.

You're a necromancer who has taken a ruined keep. Every raider, villager and crusader who dies at your walls becomes a worker. The living world escalates: more dread brings bigger crusades, which bring more bodies, which make you stronger, until a crusade is bigger than your walls.

Core loop

  1. Build crypt rooms and defenses.
  2. Survive the night's attack.
  3. Haul in the fallen and raise them by trade.
  4. Manage rot: zombies decay, skeletons last, liches are rare and expensive.
  5. Dread rises with your power, and the crusades escalate.

The new part

Attack waves are your labor supply. You want to be attacked, just not too hard.

Better use

As an unlockable faction or mode inside another concept, where it doesn't have to carry the whole game's novelty.

Side view
Moderate. Keep above, crypts below, bodies traveling down to the raising circle. It would work top-down too.
Novelty
LowDone in substance: Necromancer Accountant (corpses into minions, defend against human attacks), NecroCity (2023, undead city builder with tower defense), and Dungeons 3 and Dungeon Keeper (fallen heroes rise or convert).
Scope
Medium
Platform
Either orientation.
Risks
The least distinctive of the five. A dark theme narrows the audience on mobile storefronts. The snowball is hard to balance between runaway and death spiral.

An avatar adds controls, animation and combat feel, which roughly doubles the work before the management game is even fun. A lord unit you can take control of in a crisis gets most of the flavor.

Art and renderer

Pixel art on Canvas 2D
Canvas 2DEnough for this sprite count. The mockups run on it.
PixiJSWebGL batching and filters. Worth it for Mirrorhold's reflection shader or hundreds of units.
PhaserScenes, input and audio included. More engine than a simulation-heavy game needs.

In this genre the complexity lives in the simulation, not the renderer. Keep the model separate from drawing so you can swap renderers later.

The next two weeks

Greybox fun-tests: boxes and text, no art. Each answers one plate's "prototype asks" question.

Week 1

Hoardkeep: greed against noise

  • One mountain cross-section and five room types: forge, dorm, kitchen, tapestry hall, trap corridor.
  • The dragon as a box with a breathing timer and a stir meter.
  • Noise spreads through the room graph by breadth-first search, with falloff by distance and dampening per room.
  • One attacker type every three minutes, one enemy sap, and a wake button.

Pass: after 20 minutes you still want to push your luck, and a deliberate wake felt like a gamble.

Kill: the best play is "never run the forge", or waking is always right or always wrong.

Week 2

Countermine: detect and intercept

  • One wall, one keep, one besieger.
  • A 40×12 underground grid with fog of war. Enemy saps path toward the foundations with some randomness.
  • Listening posts return a direction and a distance band. Digging costs labor and time.
  • A fired sap drops a wall section, followed by a night assault.

Pass: interceptions feel like a hunt, and when you lose a wall you know why.

Kill: detection becomes a fixed sweep pattern, or the underground feels disconnected from the walls.

  • Model apart from rendererFixed-step simulation at about 10 ticks per second, rendering interpolated between ticks.
  • Grid plus room graphCells for rock, rooms and tunnels; A* over rooms and ladders for pathing, like Fallout Shelter's elevators.
  • One propagation routineBreadth-first search with falloff handles noise, detection and alarm in every concept.
  • Content as dataRooms, enemies and events in JSON, so balancing doesn't need code changes.
  • SavesJSON snapshots in IndexedDB, with export and import for backups.
  • ShippingA service worker for offline play; Bubblewrap to package the TWA for Play.

After the fun-tests, a vertical slice (one complete season or siege with near-final art) is my estimate of 8–10 weeks part-time. That's a guess; it depends on your hours.
