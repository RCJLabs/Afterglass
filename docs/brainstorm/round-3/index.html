Afterglass

A side-view pixel keep on the border between the living and the dead. By day the living build, craft and hold the walls. Anyone who dies wakes that night in the keep's reflection and works the night shift. This is the Mourning Glass idea from round two, worked through in depth: rules for day, dusk, night and dawn, the systems that tie them together, and how it looks in pixel art.

Six problems this design has to solve

This concept tends to break in these places. Each fix is a design proposal, untested until the greybox.

Players will get people killed on purpose.

If every death is a new worker, feeding people to the raiders becomes a strategy. Fix: how someone dies decides what wakes. Dying on duty or of old age makes a loyal shade; dying of neglect makes a Restless one that snuffs candles and turns hostile; killing your own people makes a Wraith that hunts you. Good deaths come from good lives.

Bad days spiral.

A lost raid means fewer workers by day and a flood of dead at night. Fix: mirrors cap how many shades you can bind. Extra dead wake Restless, and releasing them at dawn raises the living's morale, so a disaster turns into a recovery.

The night becomes a chore.

A second realm that only maintains the first gets skipped. Fix: night has its own verbs (light, guide, ward, hold a line in the dark) and makes things the day can't: dreams, essence, and warnings about tomorrow.

Two economies double the scope.

Fix: one set of room art serves both realms (the reflection is a vertical flip plus a palette swap), and each room has exactly one day job and one night job. A cut list is at the end.

Upside-down rooms are hard to read.

Fix: shades are dark silhouettes with glowing eyes, the camera slides down to center the reflection at night, and the interface never flips. Test it in the first art pass.

Grief is heavy material.

Fix: warm writing, names, funerals and peaceful releases, and no gore. The dawn rite is about letting go as much as keeping.

The dead-worker idea on its own is crowded: Graveyard Keeper 2, the jam game Micro Necro (ghosts of dead villagers help the living), and the upcoming My Staff Are Dead! Paranormal Tycoon. No game found moves its dead into a mirror world or has a mirror-covering rite, so those two stay at the center of everything below.

The pitch

You hold a keep on the Veil, the thin skin between the living world and the Tain, the reflected world behind every mirror. By day you're a lord of the living: you build rooms, craft goods and hold the walls against raiders. Anyone who dies inside the walls, your guard or their raider, wakes at dusk in the Tain as a shade and works the night shift in the reflected keep, holding back the Unlit that climb out of the Deep. At dawn you decide which of the dead stay bound to your mirrors and which you let rest.

Every death is a loss and a hire

A death costs the day and feeds the night. It's never only one.

Two halves, two sets of verbs

Day builds and fights at the walls. Night lights, guides and holds lines in the dark.

The mirror is the door

Every link between the realms is a mirror you hang, cover or break.

You can't farm death

A good death makes a loyal shade. Neglect and cruelty make dangerous ones.

Readable at arm's length

Fixed pixel scale, clear silhouettes, one realm in focus at a time.

About the name: Afterglass means the world after death, behind the glass. No game with that name turned up in a web search. Tainhold (after the tain, a mirror's metal backing) and Candlewake are clean backups. Names ending in "-glass" are common, so check again before you commit.

Day

Day: the living keep

The familiar half: a Fallout Shelter-style cross-section you build, staff and defend. The Tain is visible below it, dim, with its shades resting in the glass.

What you do

  • Build. Carve rooms into the keep's cross-section. Every room you build has a twin in the Tain.
  • Assign. People have skills and traits and work where you put them.
  • Craft. Tallow becomes candles, sand and silver become mirrors, ore becomes arms, grain becomes bread.
  • Defend. Walls, a gate and archers against raids. Ring the bell to send workers to their posts.
  • Tend. Heal the wounded before they die badly, and hold funerals so grief can heal.

What goes wrong by day

  • Raids by the Ashen Host, later with rams and ladders.
  • Fire spreading through wooden rooms.
  • Sickness in crowded quarters. The sick die Pale.
  • Hunger and cold. Deaths from neglect wake Restless.
  • The Lantern Church, which inspects the keep when too many souls are bound.

What last night gave you

  • Dreams: rested, happier workers.
  • Essence: healing and wards.
  • Whispers: a bound shade behind an uncovered mirror coaches whoever works that room.
  • Warnings: a black mirror shows tonight's threats and tomorrow's raid.
Dusk

Dusk: the Crossing

About thirty seconds. It's the game's signature moment, so it should look good and be short.

  • Each body in the crypt wakes as a shade, if a mirror has room for it. How the person died decides what kind of shade wakes (see People).
  • With no room left, the shade wakes Restless at the edge of the Deep. You deal with it tonight or release it at dawn.
  • You place tonight's candles. Each burns for one night; lanterns last longer but cost silver.
Night

Night: the Tain

The camera slides down to the reflection. The Tain is the same keep flipped and recolored, lit only by the candles you placed. Everything outside the light belongs to the Unlit.

What you do

  • Light. Place candles. The Unlit can't enter light, and every candle burns down by dawn.
  • Work. Shades work the twin rooms (table below).
  • Guide. Send shades where they're needed. Routes through the dark are dangerous.
  • Ward. Spend essence to seal a mirror or a rift for the night.
  • Hold. Shades fight at the edge of the light.
  • Hush. Silence every shade: the Unlit pass by, but all work stops.

What comes up from the Deep

  • Creepers: fast and weak; they snuff candles.
  • Maws: break twin rooms. A broken twin haunts its day room.
  • Weepers: seep into the Dreamwell and give the sleepers above nightmares.
  • The Hollow: a new-moon boss that eats light.
  • The Drowned: on rainy nights, up through the moat's twin.
  • Your own dead gone wrong: Restless shades and Wraiths.

DREAD

The rules

  • Keep a shade and it works tomorrow night. Dread rises by one.
  • Cover its mirror and the shade is released. The living gain remembrance, and anyone bonded to it is at peace instead of grieving.
  • Restless shades with no mirror must be released, or they turn Wraith after three nights.
  • Break a mirror in an emergency to free everyone in it at once. The room then has seven seasons of bad luck, after the old superstition about seven years.
  • Name the dead in the ledger. Named shades fade half as fast.

Dread is the cost of keeping. At five, the Lantern Church sends an inspector. Covering mirrors after a death is a real mourning custom, kept in Jewish homes during shiva and in Victorian households.

The Rite is also the save point and the natural place to stop a session.

Mirrors

Every link between the realms is a mirror. A mirror holds shades, lets the dead reach the living, and gives the Unlit a way up.

  • Capacity. Your mirrors' combined slots cap how many shades you can bind. That cap is the main brake on farming death.
  • Twins need a mirror. A twin room can only be worked if a mirror in its day room links the two.
  • Uncovered mirrors are open both ways: whispers help the living by day, and if the twin room falls at night, something comes through.
  • Cracked mirrors leak. Broken ones free their shades and curse the room.
  • The black mirror is modeled on John Dee's obsidian scrying mirror, now in the British Museum.

Bonds

  • Twinned work. A living worker and a bonded shade in a room and its twin both work a quarter faster.
  • Grief. When someone dies, the people bonded to them work a fifth slower until a funeral or a release.
  • At peace. Releasing a bonded shade turns grief into a lasting morale boost.
  • Haunting. A bonded shade that turns Wraith goes after its own kin's room first.

Fading and remembrance

Shades lose memory every night. When it's gone they pass on by themselves: free, but you lose the worker. Chapel services and memorials by day make remembrance, which restores memory, and a name in the ledger halves the fade.

Aging (a cut candidate)

The living age by season and have children. By the late game, everyone you started with works nights. It's poignant but expensive; see the cut list.

Economy

Each realm makes what the other needs. Nothing important is produced on both sides.

Seasons, moons and the calendar

  • A year is four seasons of seven days. The chart above shows the day and night split; summer builds, winter survives.
  • The seventh night of each season is a new moon. The Hollow comes up from the Deep.
  • Raiders follow the harvest. Autumn brings sieges that camp outside for days.
  • Plague summers hit crowded keeps.
  • The eclipse, once a year. Day and night run at the same time for a few minutes: both rosters awake and both threat sets attacking. It's the set piece that proves the two halves belong together.
  • The Long Night at the winter solstice ends each year: a double-length night and the year's hardest fight.
  • The Inquisition can come in any season if dread stays high.

How it looks

Everything on this page is drawn by one small pixel renderer, as a check that the style is buildable in the browser.

Technical spec

  • Canvas: 180×320 portrait, scaled by whole numbers (6× on a 1080×1920 phone) and letterboxed otherwise.
  • World: taller than the screen. The camera pans between keep and Tain, with 1× and 2× zoom so pixels stay square.
  • Grid: floors are 18 px (16 of room, 2 of floor); walls are 2 px.
  • People: 4×8 px, with two-frame walk and work cycles.
  • Palette: the 32-color ENDESGA 32 by day. The Tain is the same art flipped and run through an eight-step violet ramp.
  • Light: candlelight uses 4×4 ordered dithering, four light levels, and a slight flicker.
  • Interface: drawn at twice the world's pixel density in a pixel font, so text stays sharp.

Structure

A five-year campaign, then an open-ended sandbox. One day and night is about ten minutes, and the Rite is the save point.

The First WinterLearn the cycle; survive long nights on stockpiled candles.
The Ashen HostRaids escalate into the first siege.
The Lantern ChurchInspections, an embargo, an inquisition.
The Deep RisesThe Hollow grows; new-moon nights get worse.
The Long NightThe finale, and a choice of ending.

Endings

  • Seal the Veil. Release every shade. The Tain closes and the living go on alone.
  • Open the Veil. The living and the dead share both realms, and the keep becomes a crossing.
  • Keep the watch. Your lord dies and rules the Tain as its warden, and play continues at night.
